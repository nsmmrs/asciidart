== Welcome to Your Nutria Personal Report

[.meta]
Nutria Personal Report +

[.intro]
=== Congratulations!

This report reads a handful of your genes for what they suggest about the
vitamins and minerals your body takes up, keeps and uses. Until recently
this kind of insight was out of reach; today a small sample is enough to
look at the variants that matter for nutrition, and to turn them into
plain advice for the way you eat.

_Each of us is different in ways that a diet plan rarely considers. Some
people absorb a vitamin readily; others need more of it from food. The
pages that follow put your results side by side with what the research
says, so that you can decide what to change._

_None of this is a diagnosis: genes describe tendencies, and the way you
live shapes how much they matter._

[.intro]
== What Is Genetic Testing?

A genetic test reads a sample of cells, from saliva for example, and
looks at the places in the genome where people commonly differ. These
places, single nucleotide polymorphisms, are what the following pages
call variants; the pair of variants you carry at each place is your
genotype for it.

[.intro]
== How Are Your Results Determined?

For each nutrient we combine the genotypes of the genes studied for it
into a score, and the score into a rating: below average, normal or
above average. Some nutrients depend on one gene, others on several, each
weighted by how strongly studies have linked it with the nutrient's
levels in the body.

A rating describes a tendency, a greater or lesser chance, not a
certainty. Diet, age, health and medication all have their say as well.

== Why Is Your Genotype Important?

The studies behind this report were chosen for their size and their
care: large groups of people, measured levels of each nutrient, and
results that other groups have since confirmed. We keep that list up to
date as new work appears, and so will your report.

Advice drawn from genes is still young. What we can say with confidence
is modest, and we say no more than that; where the research is unclear,
the report tells you so.

On the following pages you will find a summary of your results, then a
page or two for each nutrient: what your genes suggest, the genes looked
at, and what you can do about it.

=== Vitamin A Tendency

image::gauge-normal.svg[Normal,role=gauge,pdfwidth=150]

.What your genes say about you:
Your genotype points to a *[.normal]#NORMAL#* ability to turn
beta-carotene from food or supplements into the active form of vitamin
A. Of the variants studied, you carry few or none of those linked with a
weaker conversion.

[.aside]
--
[.genes]
.Related genes / SNPs
****
[.gene]#BCMO1#
****

[.notes]
****
The gene studied here makes the enzyme that splits beta-carotene into
two molecules of vitamin A. Some of its common variants slow that step,
and people who carry them tend to have less of the vitamin in their
blood when they rely on plants for it.

Vitamin A matters for sight in dim light, for the skin and the linings
of the body, and for the immune system. Animal foods such as dairy, eggs
and liver provide it ready to use; plants provide carotenes that the
body converts as it needs.

Too much of the ready-made form can do harm, and pregnant women are
advised to keep foods such as liver to an occasional serving. Carotenes
from plants carry no such risk: the body converts only what it needs.

How much beta-carotene the body converts also depends on the meal: fat
helps it along, so a little oil on a salad or with cooked carrots makes
a difference, and so does cooking, which frees carotenes from the plant's
cells.

Most people in good health get enough vitamin A from a varied diet, and
the body stores what it doesn't need right away in the liver, for weeks
or months.

Orange and dark green vegetables are the richest sources of carotenes:
carrots, sweet potatoes, pumpkin, spinach and kale, as well as apricots,
mangoes and melons, and the vitamin itself is in eggs, dairy and oily
fish.
****
--

[.verdict]
====
[.lead]
Your genetic profile indicates that your response is [.normal]#NORMAL#.

[.note]
Your ability to convert beta-carotene into vitamin A is unlikely to be
reduced.
====

If you take a beta-carotene supplement, your body is likely to convert
it as expected. How your genotype affects the conversion of carotenes
from food is less clear.

'''

.Success strategies
Vitamin A needs rise during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Since you
convert beta-carotene normally, colorful vegetables and fruit are an
easy way to meet your needs, and their carotenes act as antioxidants on
the way.

=== Vitamin B9 -- Folate Tendency

image::gauge-below.svg[Below average,role=gauge,pdfwidth=150]

.What your genes say about you:
Your genotype points to a *[.below]#BELOW AVERAGE#* folate status:
you carry a variant that slows one of the steps the body uses folate
for, which can mean somewhat lower levels in the blood.

[.aside]
--
[.genes]
.Related genes / SNPs
****
[.gene]#MTHFR#
****

[.notes]
****
The enzyme this gene makes turns folate into the form the body uses to
recycle homocysteine and to build and repair DNA. One common variant
makes the enzyme less active, more so when two copies are carried.

People with the less active form may have higher homocysteine and lower
folate, especially when their diet is low in folate. Enough folate from
food usually offsets the difference.

Folate matters most before and during early pregnancy, when it helps the
baby's spine and brain develop; that is why many grains are fortified
with folic acid, the form used in supplements.

Leafy greens, legumes, asparagus, broccoli and citrus fruit are good
sources; heat destroys some of the folate in vegetables, so eat some of
them raw or lightly cooked.

Alcohol and smoking both lower folate levels. Some medicines do too:
ask your doctor or pharmacist if you take any regularly.

The variant is common: about one person in ten carries two copies, and
many more carry one. Most never notice it.
****
--

[.verdict]
====
[.lead]
Your genetic profile indicates that your response is
[.below]#BELOW AVERAGE#.

[.note]
You may have a somewhat higher chance of low folate levels. Your doctor
can measure folate and homocysteine in a routine blood test.
====

.Success strategies
* Eat folate-rich foods every day: leafy greens, beans, lentils and
  citrus fruit.
* Keep some vegetables raw or lightly steamed, as heat destroys folate.
* If you are planning a pregnancy, ask your doctor about a supplement.

[.foods,cols="1,2"]
|===
a|image::food-green.svg[Broccoli,130]
a|[.food]#Folate-rich foods to include in your diet:#

Lentils, chickpeas, asparagus, broccoli, spinach and oranges.
|===

=== Vitamin E Tendency

image::gauge-above.svg[Above average,role=gauge,pdfwidth=150]

.What your genes say about you:
Your genotype points to *[.above]#ABOVE AVERAGE#* vitamin E levels:
you carry variants linked with more of the vitamin circulating in the
blood.

[.aside]
--
[.genes]
.Related genes / SNPs
****
[.gene]#ZPR1, SCARB1, CYP4F2#
****

[.notes]
****
These genes affect how vitamin E is carried in the blood, taken up by
cells and broken down. Variants in them have been linked with the
amount of the vitamin measured in the blood of large groups of people.

Vitamin E protects fats in cell membranes from damage. Nuts, seeds and
plant oils are its richest sources; few people eat too little of it,
and supplements in high doses can interfere with blood clotting.
****
--

[.verdict]
====
[.lead]
Your genetic profile indicates that your response is
[.above]#ABOVE AVERAGE#.

[.note]
You are likely to keep good vitamin E levels from food alone.
====

.Success strategies
Since your levels tend to be good, there is little reason to add a
supplement: high doses can thin the blood. Nuts and seeds remain a
healthy source of the vitamin and of good fats.
